Best Apps to Make Friends Online in 2026, Honestly Compared

Six apps, six different theories of how strangers become friends. We rank them by the only thing that matters — how you actually end up talking to someone — and say plainly where each one (including ours) falls short.

Every app to make friends online is really a bet about where friendship starts. Swipe apps bet on a face and a bio. Event apps bet on a table. Community apps bet on a shared activity. None of them is wrong — but they suit very different people, and the reviews that rank them 1–10 rarely say which is which. So here's the comparison by mechanism, with prices and requirements stated flat.

1. Plus One — best for meeting people through interest communities

Plus One flips the usual order: you join a community around something you do — running, travel, gaming, study groups, chai chats, nightlife, house parties — and meet people inside its real-time group chat, usually by voting in a poll or saying yes to a plan. When a conversation clicks you move to 1:1; until then nobody can DM you, because first messages from non-contacts wait in Message Requests. There's a second route by place — add your college, organisation or workplace and connect with colleagues from the same one — and a third by curiosity, the worldwide Network feed.

Requirements: a username and password. No phone number, photo optional, no contact upload. Price: free on web, Android and iOS, no ads, no premium tier. Where it falls short: it's young — the community graph is densest in Indian cities and campuses, so if you're somewhere with no active community yet you'll be the one starting it. There's no built-in event ticketing; groups schedule with polls and pins instead. Live video calling is web-first today.

2. Bumble BFF — best for one-to-one swipe matching

Bumble BFF (now also a standalone Bumble For Friends app) takes the dating mechanic and makes it platonic: photos, a bio, swipe, match, chat. Its advantage is sheer pool size in large cities and mature safety tooling; a late-2025 relaunch added Groups for meeting several people at once. Requirements: photos and a phone number. Price: free with optional premium. Where it falls short: matching is front-loaded on appearance and location, conversations start cold, and many people report lots of matches that never become plans.

3. Meetup — best for in-person interest groups at scale

Meetup has run interest-based groups and events in most major cities for two decades, with tens of millions of members. You join a hiking / language / board-game group and show up. Requirements: an email; photo optional. Price: free to join and attend most events; organisers pay a subscription. Where it falls short: the chat layer is thin — conversation happens at the event, not between events — and quality depends entirely on the organiser.

4. Discord — best for large online communities and voice

Discord is a platform of servers — some private friend groups, many public communities around games, hobbies and study. Text channels, voice rooms, screen sharing, bots. Requirements: an email; no photo. Price: free with optional Nitro. Where it falls short: big public servers can be chaotic and hard to break into, text isn't end-to-end encrypted, and it's built for communities rather than for turning a community member into a friend.

5. Timeleft — best for a scheduled dinner with strangers

Timeleft matches you with five strangers by personality and books a table on Wednesday. It's the most structured option here and the fastest route to an actual face-to-face conversation. Requirements: a personality questionnaire; no photo. Price: per dinner or a subscription, plus your food. Where it falls short: it's city-dependent, costs money, and after dinner the group usually needs somewhere else to keep talking.

6. Slowly — best for slow, long-distance friendships

Slowly is a pen-pal app: letters take hours or days to arrive depending on distance, matched by shared interests and language. Requirements: no photo — an avatar. Price: free; Slowly Plus optional. Where it falls short: by design there's no real-time chat and no group, so it's for one deep correspondence at a time, not a circle.

Which should you actually install?

  • You'd rather talk about something than about yourself: Plus One (communities) or Meetup (events).
  • You want volume and don't mind cold starts: Bumble BFF.
  • You want one guaranteed in-person night: Timeleft — then move the table into a Plus One GC so the group survives dessert.
  • You're already deep in a hobby: Discord for the giant community, Plus One for the smaller crew you actually plan with.
  • You want one thoughtful friend far away: Slowly.

The pattern behind every one of these: apps get you into the room; showing up in the same room more than once makes the friend. Pick the app whose room you'd return to on a Tuesday when you're tired.

Friendship apps, answered

What is the best app to make friends online?

Depends on how you like to meet people: Bumble BFF for 1:1 swipe volume, Meetup for in-person hobby groups, Discord for big online communities, Timeleft for a scheduled dinner, Plus One for meeting people through interest communities and group chat first — free, with no photo or phone number.

Are there free apps to make friends online?

Yes. Plus One is free with no ads or paid tier. Bumble BFF, Discord and Slowly are free with optional paid extras. Meetup is free to attend most events but charges organisers. Timeleft charges per dinner or subscription.

Which friendship apps don't need a phone number or photo?

Plus One: username and password only, photo optional. Discord: email. Slowly: avatar, no photo. Bumble BFF requires photos and a phone number.

Do apps to make friends actually work?

When they put you in a shared context and you return to it. Matching alone produces first messages; activities and recurring rooms produce friends. See how to make online friends for the habits that do the converting.
